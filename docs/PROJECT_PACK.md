## 1. Executive summary

Bangla Adventures is a child-friendly web experience for children living outside Bangladesh who speak, understand, or are beginning to learn Bangla as a second or heritage language. Its purpose is to make language practice feel connected to identity, family, place, and culture rather than like a worksheet.

The platform uses a river journey led by a friendly tiger. Children can follow 18 complete lessons across six child- and heritage-adapted proficiency bands (Pre-A1, A1, A2, B1, B2 and a C1–C2 extension), hear every target word and model sentence, practise with guided tasks, complete checks, watch a curated lesson video, open a related playlist, read a bilingual story, explore culture postcards, play a word-matching game, and collect stars. The visual system combines a warm river storybook, a postcard passport collage, and a Bangladesh quest map.

The core platform deliberately avoids accounts, chat, profiling, location access, and collection of personal information. Progress is stored only on the current device. Third-party YouTube media is click-to-load, uses privacy-enhanced embeds, and carries an adult-supervision notice. This keeps the first validation round simple while the client, families, educators, and Bangladeshi cultural reviewers shape the public product.

## 2. Product hypothesis

If young heritage-language learners can encounter small amounts of Bangla in short, forgiving, culturally meaningful activities, then they are more likely to practise voluntarily, ask family members questions, and build positive associations with the language and Bangladesh.

### Value proposition

For children living outside Bangladesh, Bangla Adventures is an interactive language-and-culture journey that pairs understandable English support with spoken and written Bangla. Unlike a drill-heavy vocabulary app, it uses stories, real places, and low-pressure play to connect words with identity and curiosity.

### Success signals for the next validation round

- Children can explain what to do on the first screen without adult instruction.
- At least 80% of test participants complete one word, the story question, and one game round.
- Children voluntarily replay Bangla audio or repeat a word aloud.
- Children can recall at least two target words and use one model pattern from their selected lesson after a short break.
- Parents and educators describe the cultural framing as respectful, useful, and age-appropriate.
- No participant is asked for personal information in the child journey.

These are proposed validation measures, not claims about current outcomes.

## 3. Research findings and design implications

### 3.1 Bilingual scaffolding

UNESCO explains that learning in a language a child understands supports reading and comprehension and can make additional language learning easier. It also stresses that multilingual digital tools should reflect cultural context and community needs.

**Design response:** every learning moment can show Bangla and English together; Bangla can be heard again; no accent is treated as a failure; and cultural context is integral rather than decorative.

Source: [UNESCO — What you need to know about multilingual education](https://www.unesco.org/en/languages-education/need-know)

### 3.2 Language, culture, and Bangladesh

International Mother Language Day is observed on 21 February. UNESCO states that the idea was an initiative of Bangladesh, approved by its 1999 General Conference, and observed worldwide since 2000.

**Design response:** language is framed as a source of identity and connection. The culture passport uses the date “21 · 02” as a quiet visual reference, while the product avoids turning a historically significant day into a points mechanic.

Source: [UNESCO — International Mother Language Day](https://www.unesco.org/en/days/mother-language)

### 3.3 Grounded cultural content

UNESCO currently lists three World Heritage properties in Bangladesh: the Historic Mosque City of Bagerhat, the Ruins of the Buddhist Vihara at Paharpur, and the Sundarbans.

**Design response:** the first three culture postcards use these real places. Copy remains brief and wonder-led, with a clear plan for community and specialist review before public release.

Source: [UNESCO World Heritage Convention — Bangladesh](https://whc.unesco.org/en/statesparties/bd)

### 3.4 Safety by design

Australia’s eSafety Commissioner describes Safety by Design as putting user safety and rights at the centre of product development and identifying harms before release. Its principles include service-provider responsibility, user empowerment and autonomy, and transparency and accountability.

**Design response:** the platform removes unnecessary risk surfaces instead of relying on child settings. There is no chat, public profile, user-generated content, contact between users, or first-party data-sharing workflow. Curated external media stays disconnected until a learner deliberately loads it and is clearly identified as third-party content.

Source: [eSafety Commissioner — Safety by Design](https://www.esafety.gov.au/industry/safety-by-design)

### 3.5 Children’s privacy in Australia

The OAIC says the Children’s Online Privacy Code will apply to relevant online services likely to be accessed by children and will add requirements for handling children’s personal information. The OAIC page was updated on 21 July 2026 and states that the final Code must be registered by 10 December 2026.

**Design response:** collect no personal information in the child prototype; keep progress device-local; avoid third-party advertising and behavioural analytics; prepare a privacy impact assessment and legal review before any public launch or account feature.

This pack is product guidance, not legal advice.

Source: [OAIC — Children’s Online Privacy Code](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-registers/privacy-codes/childrens-online-privacy-code)

### 3.6 Cognitive accessibility

W3C guidance recommends easy words, short sentences, clear images, whitespace, visible labels, familiar controls, feedback, and recovery from mistakes.

**Design response:** one main task per section, short instructions, large labelled buttons, no timers, replayable audio, visible progress, positive retry messages, conventional modal close controls, keyboard access, and reduced-motion support.

Source: [W3C — Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)

### 3.7 Existing language resources

The University of Iowa curates Bengali language and culture resources spanning dictionaries, reading and writing, pronunciation, and listening and speaking.

**Design response:** each of the 18 lessons now includes a reviewed-for-relevance YouTube video and curated playlist. Embeds are click-to-load, use YouTube’s privacy-enhanced domain, identify the source channel, and warn that external recommendations remain under YouTube’s control. Content and rights review remains required before broad public release.

Source: [University of Iowa — Bengali Language and Culture Resources](https://clcl.uiowa.edu/language-resources/bengali-language-and-culture-resources)

### 3.8 Proficiency progression for young heritage learners

The Council of Europe’s CEFR uses six broad levels from A1 to C2, allows levels to be adapted or subdivided for local contexts, and publishes supplemental Pre-A1 and young-learner descriptors. Those descriptors are useful as a shared “can do” language, but they do not automatically become an assessment simply by being placed in an app.

**Design response:** the platform uses Pre-A1, A1, A2, B1, B2 and a combined C1–C2 extension as transparent pathway labels. Pre-A1 through B1 is the core child pathway, B2 is a stretch band, and C1–C2 is an advanced heritage project band likely to need family or educator support. The two-minute level guide is explicitly non-diagnostic, and learners are encouraged to move bands based on comfort and demonstrated use rather than age.

Sources: [Council of Europe — CEFR level descriptions](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/level-descriptions) and [supplementary descriptors for young learners](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/bank-of-supplementary-descriptors)

## 5. Experience principles

1. **Bangla is an invitation, not a test.** Children may listen, repeat, or read without a timer.
2. **English supports comprehension.** It should not replace meaningful exposure to Bangla.
3. **Culture is specific and reviewed.** Use real places, stories, people, arts, food, nature, and everyday life; avoid a token collage of symbols.
4. **Small wins are visible.** Stars show progress but do not rank children or create pressure.
5. **Safety is the default.** Remove unnecessary data and social features.
6. **Adults can understand the system.** Privacy and progress are explained plainly.
7. **Access is part of quality.** Keyboard, touch, readable structure, audio replay, and reduced motion are core requirements.

## 6. Implemented platform scope

### Child journey

- Bilingual English/Bangla interface switch
- Quest-map hero with clear first action
- Six proficiency bands: Pre-A1, A1, A2, B1, B2 and C1–C2 heritage extension
- 18 complete lessons with “can do” outcomes, objectives, vocabulary, model patterns, explicit teaching, cultural context, three guided activities, a family mission, and two-question check
- Two-minute starting-level guide based on demonstrated language use rather than age
- One relevant YouTube video and one curated playlist integrated into every lesson with a privacy-conscious loading gate
- Bundled fallback narration for all 108 curriculum vocabulary items and 36 model sentences
- Four Bangla word cards: নদী, নৌকা, বাঘ, বন্ধু
- Device Bangla speech when available, with compact bundled Ogg narration when it is not
- Word examples and practice completion
- Three-page bilingual story, “Maya and the moonlit boat”
- Story comprehension question with retry feedback
- Culture passport cards for the Sundarbans, Bagerhat, and Paharpur
- Four-pair English/Bangla matching game
- Stars and activity progress stored in local browser storage

### Adult journey

- Grown-ups panel with activity completion and privacy explanation
- Device-local reset control
- Source links to UNESCO and the Council of Europe CEFR framework
- Plain explanation that the proficiency path is an adaptation, not an official CEFR assessment
- Explicit warning that cultural and language content needs co-review before public release

### Deliberately out of scope for the first validation build

- Child accounts, passwords, email, profiles, avatars, chat, comments, leaderboards, sharing, or friend systems
- Advertising, behavioural profiling, targeted recommendations, or third-party analytics
- Payment, subscription, or commerce
- Teacher classroom administration
- Cloud-synced progress
- User-generated content
- Automated voice grading
- Automatic YouTube loading, autoplay, or an algorithmic in-platform video feed

## 8. Non-functional requirements

### Accessibility

- Target WCAG 2.2 AA for the public release.
- Maintain keyboard-visible focus and semantic headings, regions, buttons, and dialogs.
- Provide meaningful alternative text for informative images.
- Never require audio alone; all spoken learning content has visible text.
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Use no time limits in the child journey.
- Validate Bengali font rendering across Chrome, Safari, Edge, Android, and iOS.

### Privacy and safety

- Data minimisation: no personal information in the prototype.
- No first-party advertising, tracking pixels, location, camera, microphone capture, or public interaction.
- YouTube remains disconnected until the learner opts in; the interface discloses the external privacy boundary and recommends adult supervision for younger children.
- Adult-facing explanation of progress storage and reset.
- Complete a privacy impact assessment before accounts, analytics, or cloud progress.
- Re-check the final Australian Children’s Online Privacy Code after registration and before release.

### Performance

- Optimise production illustrations to modern image formats before a large public launch.
- Set a target Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds on a representative mid-range mobile connection.
- Lazy-load below-the-fold illustration assets in a future performance pass.

### Reliability

- Core reading and visual activities remain usable if speech synthesis is unavailable.
- Storage failures should not block the current session.
- Core lesson reading, teaching, practice, audio, and checks do not depend on a third-party API; videos are optional enrichment.

### Compatibility

- Responsive layout from small phones to desktop.
- Touch targets designed for children.
- Current versions of Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge; confirm the final support matrix with the client.

## 10. Technical architecture

### Current platform

```text
Child or adult browser
        |
        v
Next/Vinext client experience
  |-- bilingual content and interactions
  |-- 18-lesson curriculum and proficiency filters
  |-- device speech plus 165 bundled audio files
  |-- device-local progress (localStorage)
  |-- static storybook illustrations
  `-- opt-in YouTube privacy-enhanced embeds and playlists

No database · no login · no personal-data API · no third-party analytics
```

### Recommended public-product evolution

1. Keep the child journey statically rendered where possible for speed and safety.
2. Add a reviewed content-management workflow accessible only to authorised adults.
3. Store published lessons separately from drafts with immutable review metadata.
4. Use human-recorded Bangla audio in object storage and a CDN.
5. Introduce optional adult-managed cloud progress only after consent, identity, retention, deletion, and child-privacy requirements are resolved.
6. Add privacy-preserving aggregate analytics only when there is a defined research question and documented lawful basis.

The client has offered cloud account access/credits and domain/hosting support. Final AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, or Oracle selection should follow the client’s existing environment, team expertise, safeguarding requirements, and cost model rather than being decided before the first technical workshop.

## 11. Security and privacy threat sketch

| Risk | Current mitigation | Public-release action |
|---|---|---|
| Collection of child identifiers | No account or form | Privacy impact assessment before any identity feature |
| Unsafe child-to-child or adult-to-child contact | No social or messaging features | Formal safety case before adding communication |
| Behavioural tracking | No analytics in prototype | Data map, lawful basis, consent and retention decision |
| Cultural misinformation | UNESCO grounding and limited scope | Community, educator, historian and language review |
| Inappropriate third-party content | Explicitly curated videos, click-to-load gate, privacy-enhanced embed, no in-platform feed, adult-supervision notice | Maintain a reviewed allowlist, replacement process, and periodic link/content audit |
| Excessive reward pressure | Stars only; no timer, streak, rank, or purchase | Test wellbeing impact with children and carers |
| Shared-device privacy | Progress is anonymous and resettable | Clearly explain storage; add adult reset and retention rules |
| Supply-chain or application vulnerability | Minimal dependency surface | Dependency scanning, headers, penetration test, incident plan |

## 13. Proposed roadmap

### Phase 0 — Client alignment (week 1)

- Confirm outcomes, age range, target countries, vocabulary standard, and cultural governance.
- Identify content owner, safeguarding owner, technical owner, and approval process.
- Decide review participants and prototype test plan.

### Phase 1 — Content and design validation (weeks 2–4)

- Test this private prototype with 5–8 children and accompanying adults.
- Review Bangla, transliteration, story, illustrations, and cultural facts.
- Review and refine the implemented six-band curriculum progression and content style guide.
- Resolve brand name, identity, and domain.

### Phase 2 — Production hardening (weeks 5–8)

- Replace synthetic fallback speech with reviewed human recordings while keeping the same reliable fallback delivery model.
- Add content publication workflow and asset rights records.
- Complete accessibility, privacy, security, and performance work.
- Complete professional review of all 18 lessons, YouTube resources, transliteration, and cultural framing; add printable/classroom extensions where evidence supports them.

### Phase 3 — Pilot (weeks 9–11)

- Invite a small, consented family/community-school cohort.
- Observe completion, comprehension, voluntary replay, and qualitative feedback.
- Fix content and usability issues; avoid adding features without evidence.

### Phase 4 — Launch decision (week 12)

- Review pilot evidence, safeguarding and privacy readiness, support plan, cost, and content capacity.
- Decide whether to launch publicly, run another pilot, or narrow scope.

## 16. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| “Children outside Bangladesh” is too broad for one pathway | High | High | Select a primary age, language baseline, and pilot geography |
| Cultural content becomes superficial or contested | Medium | High | Establish paid/recognised community review and source records |
| Synthetic pronunciation needs professional review | High | Medium | Use the bundled fallback for functional reliability, then replace it with human-reviewed recordings for public production |
| Accounts create privacy and safeguarding complexity | Medium | High | Keep child journey anonymous until a clear need and lawful design exist |
| Reward mechanics distract from intrinsic interest | Medium | Medium | No streaks/rank/purchases; test stars with children and carers |
| Scope expands faster than content can be reviewed | High | High | Content governance and definition of done for every lesson |
| Accessibility is deferred | Medium | High | Include audit and disabled-user testing before public release |
| Cloud choice is made before requirements | Medium | Medium | Choose after identity, content workflow, regions, budget, and support are known |

## 17. Definition of done for a public MVP

- Client has approved a written scope, target users, and success measures.
- All Bangla, English, transliteration, audio, illustrations, and cultural facts have named reviewers and approval records.
- Child and adult journeys meet agreed WCAG 2.2 AA acceptance criteria.
- Privacy impact assessment, data map, retention/deletion rules, and legal review are complete.
- Safety risk assessment and incident/escalation process are documented.
- No child-facing advertising, profiling, unsafe contact, or dark patterns.
- Browser/device, performance, security, and recovery testing pass.
- Pilot evidence supports comprehension, usability, safety, and cultural quality.
- Content, technical, support, and safeguarding owners accept handover.
